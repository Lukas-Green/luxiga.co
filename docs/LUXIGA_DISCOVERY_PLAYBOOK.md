# LUXIGA Discovery Playbook — "Price by Pain of Inaction"
> Date: 2026-05-21 | Audience: Lukas (founder sales asset) | Origin: Lukas teaching Justin, captured in `GGC dumps 5.txt`

## TL;DR
A four-move discovery framework Lukas already uses intuitively. The summary version: **define success with the prospect first, get them to name what happens if the problem isn't solved, anchor price to that pain (not to your hours), and mirror their language back.** This document captures the method as a reusable sales asset — scripts, listen-fors, traps, and pre-call prep — so LUXIGA can run it consistently and SCRIBE/SCOUT agents can lean on it when drafting outbound.

---

## Why this method beats the default

Most freelancers and solo founders price by **scope of work** — hours billed, deliverables shipped, features implemented. That traps you in commodity work. Whoever bids the lowest hours wins, and the buyer never appreciates the value because you anchored the conversation in your effort instead of their outcome.

LUXIGA's method inverts this. The price gets anchored to **what the prospect already loses by not solving the problem.** That number is almost always 5-10x larger than the prospect would have proposed, and they arrive at it themselves — so they own it.

Lukas's own words: *"This is where a bunch of freelancers get fucked up."*

## The Four Moves

### Move 1 — Define success metrics with the prospect first

**What to do:** Before talking about what you'd build, get them to commit to a specific definition of success. Don't accept vague answers.

**Script lines:**
- "Before we talk about what I'd build, I want to make sure we're solving the right problem. What does success look like for you in 90 days?"
- "If we did this work and it nailed everything you hoped — how would you know? What would be measurably different?"
- "Walk me through the metric you'd watch to know this was working."

**Listen for:**
- Specific numbers ("we'd want 20 more leads/month")
- Named people ("our ops manager wouldn't be drowning")
- Deadline references ("before the busy season hits in August")
- Emotional language ("I'm tired of...")

**Write down:**
- The metric (exact words)
- The timeline (exact words)
- Who's affected (names, roles)
- Anything they said with energy — those words come back later

**Why this matters:** Most discovery calls start with "tell me about your business." That's wrong. It puts the prospect in pitch-to-Lukas mode. The right opener puts them in name-the-outcome mode.

---

### Move 2 — Lead them to articulate the cost of inaction

**What to do:** Once success is defined, flip the frame. Ask what happens if it doesn't get solved. Sit in the silence. Let them say the number.

**Script lines (pick the right one for energy in the room):**

If they're analytical:
- "If you do nothing about this for six more months, what changes? What's the realistic cost?"

If they're emotional:
- "What does this cost you when you can't sleep on it? When you're driving home thinking about it?"

If they're skeptical:
- "Let's stress-test this. If we both walk away from this conversation today and nothing changes — what's the actual business consequence?"

If they're a procurement person:
- "What's the budget impact of letting this run another quarter? Both direct cost and opportunity cost."

**The follow-up pivot (always):**
- "Say more about that."
- "How many [units / people / dollars / hours] is that, ballpark?"
- "And what's the multiplier? If that happens once, does it cost more the second time?"

**Listen for:**
- Specific dollar amounts ("losing maybe $5K/month")
- Escalation language ("getting worse," "compounding," "snowballing")
- Named consequences ("we'd lose the contract with X")
- Stress signals ("I'm already losing sleep over this")

**The anchoring move:** Whatever number they name, you don't have to challenge it. You write it down and reflect it back. *"So six months of not solving this costs you around $30K, give or take?"* — wait for confirmation. That number is now their anchor.

---

### Move 3 — Anchor price to pain, not scope

**What to do:** When you eventually quote, the price should reference *their* cost-of-inaction number — not your hours, not your scope, not what you charged last time.

**The math (rough heuristic):**
- If their stated 6-month cost-of-inaction is **$X**, your reasonable price point is **$X / 2 to $X / 3** for the work that solves it.
- This sounds expensive in absolute terms and **cheap relative to their own stated number.**
- Half their pain saved = price they'll accept. One-third of their pain saved = price they're excited about.

**Counter-example (what NOT to do):**
- They say six months of pain = $30K
- You estimate 40 hours of work
- You quote $4,000 ($100/hr)
- You leave $11K-$15K on the table AND you've signaled the work is commodity-cheap. They'll commoditize you on the next gig too.

**Script for delivering the anchored price:**
- "You mentioned this is costing you about [their number]. The work I'd do here lands you at success in roughly [their timeline]. My fee is [X], which is about [X / their-number] of the cost of leaving this alone."
- Then *stop talking.* Don't justify. Don't add features. Let them respond.

**Listen for:**
- "That's fair." → close.
- Silence + a nod → close.
- "That's a lot." → ask "compared to what?" Lead them back to their own number.
- "Can we phase it?" → yes, but don't cut the total. Phase the deliverables, not the price.

---

### Move 4 — Use the language they used

**What to do:** When you write the proposal, the recap email, the SOW, the contract — every key noun, verb, and adjective should be theirs, not yours.

**Why:** Buyers don't read carefully. They scan. When they see their own language reflected back, the brain registers "this person heard me" before the analytical brain even engages with the substance. That feeling carries the deal across the line.

**Practical mechanic:**
- During the call, write verbatim quotes in a `quotes:` block at the bottom of your notes
- When drafting the proposal, the headline = a near-quote
- Section headers = their words for the problem
- Deliverables = framed in their success language
- Pricing rationale = references their cost-of-inaction number directly

**What to remove:**
- Industry jargon they didn't use
- Your favorite vocabulary
- Any word that sounds clever but might confuse
- "Synergize," "leverage," "robust," "scalable" — kill these unless they used them first

## Common failure modes

### Trap 1 — Talking before they've named the cost

Symptom: you start pitching solutions before Move 2 lands.
Cost: price reverts to your hours; you lose the anchor.
Fix: stay silent longer. Resist the urge to fill space.

### Trap 2 — Negotiating against your own price

Symptom: they ask "can you do it for less?" and you immediately propose a cheaper option.
Cost: you've signaled your number was inflated; they'll push harder.
Fix: reflect their cost-of-inaction number back instead. "If we phase it, you absorb three more months of [their stated cost]. Is that the trade you want?"

### Trap 3 — Adding deliverables instead of holding price

Symptom: they hesitate on price; you add features to "make it worth it."
Cost: scope creep before kickoff; lower hourly rate; resentment within 30 days.
Fix: hold the price. If you must move, move the timeline (slower delivery) or the payment terms (phased payment), never the scope.

### Trap 4 — Skipping Move 1 because you "already know"

Symptom: prospect is a referral or repeat client; you assume you know their success metric.
Cost: you build the wrong thing; they're unhappy at delivery; they don't refer you.
Fix: run Move 1 anyway. *"Even though we've worked together before, I want to make sure I have the current definition of success — what's different now?"*

## Practice prompts (run with Claude or a friend before a real call)

### Scenario 1 — The skeptical contractor

> Justin's contractor friend asks: "Why would I pay $1,500/mo for software that just helps me talk to my Spanish-speaking crew? I already have a guy."
>
> Run Moves 1-4. Goal: get him to name what his current interpreter costs (including time-lost-translating, mis-translations, double-trips back to site).

### Scenario 2 — The non-buyer

> A small property manager says they're "just exploring." They have 8 commercial roofs in their portfolio.
>
> Run Moves 1-2. Goal: NOT to close. Goal is to learn whether they have a real problem worth solving, or whether they're tire-kicking. Use Move 1 to find out within 5 minutes.

### Scenario 3 — The price objection mid-conversation

> Mid-call, the prospect says "I have a guy who does this for $40/hr." Their cost-of-inaction is $20K over 6 months.
>
> Don't drop your price. Don't argue. Run a reframe that returns the conversation to their own stated cost.

```
   "Before we talk about what I'd build —
    what does success look like in 90 days?"

   "If you do nothing for 6 more months —

   Their pain ÷ 2 or 3 = your price.
   Reference their number when quoting.
   Stop talking after the price.

4. MIRROR THEIR LANGUAGE
   Every heading + deliverable in the proposal
   uses their words, not yours.

DON'T:
   - Pitch before Move 2 lands
   - Negotiate against your own number
   - Add scope to hold price
   - Skip Move 1 with a repeat client
```

## Where this overlaps with LUXIGA's wizard UX principle

Both lead the user through a discovery flow with clear confirmation at each step:
- Move 1 = the "what are you trying to accomplish?" intake wizard step
- Move 2 = the "tell us what's at stake" clarifying-question step
- Move 3 = the price-reveal modal with the anchored rationale
- Move 4 = the recommendations + confirmation feedback at output time

LUXIGA's product UX and LUXIGA's sales motion are the same shape. That's not a coincidence — they're both Lukas's instinct for "lead the user, don't drop them into ambiguity."
